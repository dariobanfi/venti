# Design

Last revised: 2026-11-22

How Venti looks and behaves, on every platform. The iOS app is the first place these rules are built; Android and the web follow the same structure, colours and wording with their own platform's components. The web app is the second and the Android app the third; what is particular to each is under [Web](#web) and [Android](#android).

A change to a screen that contradicts this document updates it in the same commit.

## The design language

Since 2026-11-20 the product's look is the owner's design language, "Valley", in [`design/`](../design/DESIGN.md): cream paper and indigo ink, coral for the one action that starts or records a flight, teal for sink and coral and gold for lift, Fira Sans, a set of flying icons, the spiral logo and the valley art, in three themes: `light`, `dark`, and `flight` from take-off to landing. [`design/DESIGN.md`](../design/DESIGN.md) is the rule for colour, type, shape, components, the map's colours, flight mode, the logo and the voice, and [`design/reference/`](../design/reference/index.html) shows it rendered. This document goes on saying what each screen holds and does, and where a platform differs.

**What it changes here.** The principles below were written for a look made of the platforms' own unstyled components. They still hold for behaviour: the platform's navigation, sheets, lists, menus and gestures are used as they are, so that the text size, the contrast settings and the screen readers keep working. What they wear is the tokens': colours, type, radii and spacing come from `design/tokens`, never from a screen. Where a principle below and `DESIGN.md` disagree about a look, `DESIGN.md` is right and this document is to be corrected. Three such places are known: translucent glass over the map (principle 1; the pack says no glass, and a shadow only for what floats over the map), the rounded system face for numbers (principle 4; the pack sets them in Fira Sans with tabular figures), and the one purple accent (principle 6; the pack's primary is indigo and its one accent coral).

**How an app takes it.** From `design/` at build time, as the apps take the sample flight and the map style from where the repository keeps them: the tokens, the fonts, the icons, the app icon and the art are not copied into an app's folder. The pack's `VentiTheme.swift` and `VentiTheme.kt` had not been compiled when the pack arrived.

**Where the apps stand.** The iPhone app wears a first pass of it since 2026-11-20: the pack's tokens compiled in, Fira Sans, the app icon, the light and the dark theme, its ground and surfaces under every list, its buttons, its ink in three steps, its surface with a shadow in place of glass over the map, its vario scale of seven steps for climb and sink, and its icons in the tab bar for the map, flying and the logbook. Since then also the cockpit as the pack's flight screen, the flight's page with the hero number, Today with the valley art and the status mark, the five tabs, the map's own colours with everything drawn on it, and the Map tab with the pack's chips, round buttons, pins and site sheet. A screen that is empty because nothing has happened there yet (no flights, no waypoints, no public flights, nobody in the air) shows the pack's valley with what is missing and what brings it; something that went wrong is said with the caution sign and without art. The sheets the cockpit opens keep to the flight mode's sizes: no text under 14, rows 64 high, the lesser ink as a role of the theme, and a climb's colour as a mark beside its number, not as the number's colour. Not yet on the iPhone: the wind's seven steps by speed (the core sorts wind into four bands, which take four of the pack's seven), and a dark map. The Android app wears it since the same day, and so does the web app, each as its section ([Android](#android), [Web](#web)) says, with what is still open there. Bringing it in is, for each app, the tokens and fonts, the app icon and logo, the three themes, and then screen by screen against `design/reference/screens/`. A screen is converted whole or not at all, and until then keeps the look it has.

**Open between the pack and this document**, for the owner: the pack's tab bar (Today, Map, Fly, Flights, League; its own section 10 calls the names placeholders) against the apps' sections (Map, Fly, Logbook, Assistant and Settings, and Plans on the web); its airspace colours and scale breaks, which it marks as proposals, against the colours [airspace.md](airspace.md) describes; and in flight "at most two buttons" and "ending a flight is press and hold" against today's cockpit.

## Principles

1. **The map and the flight are the content. Controls float above them.** Translucent "glass" surfaces are for navigation and controls only: bars, the flight panel, map buttons. Lists, forms and empty states use plain system backgrounds. This is Apple's own rule for its current design ("Don't use Liquid Glass in the content layer") and it is what keeps a map readable.
2. **System components first.** Tab bar, navigation bar, lists, sheets, alerts, menus and symbols are the platform's own, unstyled. They follow light and dark mode, text size, contrast and transparency settings without code of ours, and they change with the system when it changes. We draw our own component only where the platform has none: the track thumbnail, the metric, the flight panel, the climb colours.
3. **One line per flight, and it is boringly obvious.** A logbook row answers "which flight, when, how long, how far" without being opened. Flighty's designers describe their flight rows the same way: airport boards have had fifty years to work out what matters.
4. **Numbers are set, not just printed.** A value is large, in the rounded system face with fixed-width digits; its unit is small and grey beside it; what it measures is a caption below. A number that changes under the finger must not jitter sideways.
5. **Nothing important hides behind a gesture.** The headline numbers of a flight are on screen before anything is touched. Dragging along the chart adds detail; it reveals nothing essential.
6. **Colour carries meaning or it is not used.** There is one accent, one scale for climb and sink, and (later) one set of status colours for "flyable, marginal, not flyable". Text is never set in a data colour.
7. **It works with no connection and in sunlight.** Fonts are inside the app. Every screen has a state for "nothing here yet" that says what to do next. The cockpit gets its own stricter rules when it is built (below).

### Logbook

A system list grouped by year, newest first. The first row holds the totals: flights, airtime, cross-country distance. It leads to the statistics: a bar chart of the last twelve months (airtime, distance or number of flights); the years one under another, each with its flights, airtime, distance, longest flight and highest point; a bar chart of January to December over all the years, which says when in the year the pilot flies; a typical flight, which is the middle one of all for each figure, so that one long day does not speak for the rest; the kinds of flight (free flight, triangle, FAI triangle) with how many there are, how many came back to where they began and the farthest, which the row leads to; the gliders flown; and the pilot's bests, each leading to its flight. The years, the months of the year, the typical flight and the kinds are on the iPhone, Android and the web (the months of the year share the switch of the twelve months' chart). **By take-off**, worked out in the core since 2026-11-21 (`site_statistics`), and a section of the statistics on the iPhone, on Android and on the web since the same day, after the kinds of flight (on a wide screen of the web a table like the years', take-off, flights, airtime, distance, longest, farthest, first and last flown, the take-off leading to its farthest flight; the first eight places, then "All N take-offs" lists the rest in place; a row is the place's name or where it is, the flights, the airtime and the farthest flight with its day): the places the pilot's flights began at, the one flown from most often first, each with its flights, airtime, distance, the longest and the farthest, which the row leads to, and when the pilot first and last flew there. A place is where flights began within 300 m of the earliest of them; it carries the name of the pilot's waypoint there, a take-off before any other, and places of one name are one, so that two starts of one mountain the pilot calls by one name stand together. Without a waypoint a place is known by where it is, since a flight file names no place and the names the server has are OpenStreetMap's, which do not go into the logbook. Each year's header repeats that year's count and airtime. A row shows the track's shape as a thumbnail, the date, the take-off time and glider, and on the right the airtime and the scored distance, with a small triangle if the flight scored as one.

**Tendencies.** What the pilot's own tracks say about how they fly, worked out in the core since 2026-11-21 (`tendencies`); the screen, "How you fly", is specified at the end of this paragraph and written for the iPhone and Android, and on the web since the same day, seen running in its browser tests. On both phones it is seen running since 2026-11-21 in UI tests, empty with one flight (`…-6f-how-you-fly-empty`) and with observations (`…-6f-how-you-fly`) once the logbook holds five more copies of the sample flight on other days, which is how the web's browser test fills it. Those are copies of one made-up flight, so what the screenshots say is made up too, and three of the lines read "0 of 10", "0 s" and "0 m": true of that flight, and a wording to look at with real ones. Each flight's figures are counted when it is imported, by the code that breaks a flight into thermals and glides (`venti_flight::habits`), and kept with the flight in the logbook's index; a flight imported before that gets them the first time the tendencies are asked for. The rules: a thermal was circled left or right when the track's direction turned at least one full circle (360°) net that way and at least two thirds of all its turning went that way, with fixes closer than 15 m together left out, since a GPS track jitters by a few metres and over short steps that counts as turning both ways; any other was flown back and forth, in eights or beats along a slope or with a change of direction, or was too short to say, and does not count for the direction (both limits were set on 2026-11-21 against some 660 thermals of the owner's own flights, counted outside the repository: two thirds of them are then one way, where the first limits, two circles and 80%, left more than half undecided); how a thermal ended is measured on thermals of at least two minutes that climbed at least 0.5 m/s on average, and one "ended weak" when its last minute climbed at less than a third of its own average; the time after the top is from the moment such a thermal's highest point was first reached to its exit, and the height below the top is from that highest point down to the exit; the best thermal is chosen among those of a minute or more; glides count from a kilometre in a straight line that lost height, and the glide ratio is their distance over their height lost, together; where a flight ended is the straight distance from the first to the last point of its track (the ends of the outline the logbook keeps), and one that ended within a kilometre of that ended where it began. Over the logbook the core says eight things, each only with enough behind it and most with a flight that shows it most, to open, in words such as these: which way the pilot circles ("Of 409 thermals circled one way, 337 went right and 72 left. 252 more were flown back and forth or were too short to say.", from ten thermals circled one way, with the flight that has the most thermals circled the rarer way), how their thermals end ("In 3 of 11 thermals the last minute climbed at less than a third of the thermal's average.", from ten measured, with the flight that has most), the time after the top ("On average 48 s between a thermal's highest point and leaving it.", with the flight of the longest average over at least three), leaving a thermal ("On average 35 m below a thermal's highest point when you leave it.", over the same measured thermals, with the flight of the largest average over at least three), climbing ("1.4 m/s on average over 12300 m gained in thermals; the best thermal gave 3.1 m/s.", from ten thermals, with the flight of the best thermal), gliding ("7.2 to 1 over 86 km in 54 glides of a kilometre or more.", from ten glides) and the share of the airtime spent circling ("38% of your airtime was spent in thermals.", from two hours of airtime), and last where the flights end ("23 of 45 flights ended within a kilometre of where they began; the farthest ended 44 km away.", from ten flights, with the bar the share of the first number in the second and the flight that ended farthest away). Of flights that show something equally, the newest is named. The words say what was measured and nothing else: observations, never a verdict on the pilot, no praise, no "too long" and no "should", and never advice about what is safe; a note at the foot says that they are counted from the pilot's own GPS tracks by fixed rules and are not a judgement of the pilot or of what is safe. What is weak in it, to verify: every limit, with pilots; a climb over one minute is noisy by a few tenths of a metre per second at one fix a second, so "ended weak" is uncertain in weak thermals; the analysis finds a thermal's exit within some seconds of where the circling ended, which counts a few seconds of the glide after as time after the top; a glide is measured in a straight line from its start to its end, which makes a glide that turned look worse; the height below the top takes in the few seconds of glide that the exit includes, some metres of sink; a flight out and back, or a flight to a landing beside the take-off's mountain, ends "where it began", which says nothing of why it ended there; and nothing here knows the air the pilot was in, what they meant to do or the pilots beside them. **On the screen:** the statistics end with a row "How you fly", which leads to a screen of that name. It asks the core off the main thread, since an older logbook is counted flight by flight the first time (some 0.15 s a flight on a computer, more on a phone), and says "Counting your flights" with the pack's progress sign meanwhile. Then each observation is a card: its title, its words, a thin bar in the primary colour where the core gives a share (the words beside it say what the share is of, so the bar is never the only bearer), and, where the core names a flight, a row "The flight that shows it most" with its day, which opens that flight. Under the cards the core's note, in the caption style. With nothing to say yet, one line: "Nothing to say yet. The observations begin once the logbook holds ten thermals." No colour for good or bad anywhere on it: these are observations.

With no flights the screen says so and offers two buttons: import flights (the main action) and add a sample flight. A flight's details say which airspace it entered: one row for each stretch inside airspace the app treats as to avoid or to mind, with the time, how long, between which heights, and "possibly" where a limit above ground or a flight level could not be placed; a tap marks the entry on the map, and the flight's map draws the airspace around it. Under the list it says what the check is worth: the airspace the app has today, not that of the day of the flight, nothing about what was active, a help for the pilot's own review. Importing takes single files or a whole folder, with the folders inside it: a pilot's history in one go. While many files are read, a bar over the bottom of the list counts them ("Importing 37 of 312") and the list fills; at the end one sentence says how many flights were added and how many were there already. Files in a folder that are not flight files are passed over without a word.

Deleting asks first. A flight's file exists only on the device, so it cannot be undone.

**Everyone's flights.** With a Venti server set, the League tab on the phones has a switch: "Everyone" and "Ranking"; on the web the Logbook has one in its title bar: "Mine", "Everyone" and "Ranking". Ranking lists the server's pilots by the points of their best three public flights, as the web app does ([the ranking](#web)): a row of years over the list, each pilot a group of rows with the place, the name, what counts and the points, and under it the flights that count, which open like any public flight; the pilot's row leads to that pilot's flights. Everyone is the list of flights the pilots of that server share with everyone, as XContest lists what was flown: the newest first, or the best by points, chosen above the list. A row has the track's shape, the pilot's name, the day and the glider, and on the right the scored distance, with a triangle marked, and the airtime. More is fetched as the end of the list comes into view. A flight opens like one of the pilot's own, on the map with its profile, its numbers and its thermals, from its file, which is fetched for that and not kept; the title is the pilot's name, and its menu leads to everything else that pilot shares with everyone and hands on the flight's page on the server. Under the list it says whose reckoning the distances and points are. A take-off's page on the map lists the same flights from its side: "Flown from here", the five newest public flights that took off within a kilometre and a half of it, each opening the same way; a site nobody shared a flight from has no such section. Following, cheers and comments are below; there is no league yet.

Fetched when the screen opens; nothing is pushed.
  - *On the screens* (built in the three apps on 2026-11-22 from the proposal of four plain places and no settings page of their own; every word, line and refusal is the core's, `social_words()`). Signed out, a pilot sees the counts and the lines and never a control.

**Flying plans.** Who means to fly where on which day, among pilots who know each other. The row "Flying plans" of the map's layers sheet (a round button with a calendar until 2026-11-22) opens the board: the plans of every group the pilot is in, day by day from today on ("Today", "Tomorrow", then the weekday and date), each a line with the place, the time if it has one, who is in ("You, Mara Muster and Jonas Beispiel") and its group, and a tick where the pilot is in. A plan's page says everything it holds, lists who is in, and has the pilot's own word on it: "I am in" or "I am out". Its author changes it; its author and the group's keeper delete it. A new plan is a short form: where (a name the pilot types), the day, a time if wanted, a note; with more than one group, which. Under the board, "Groups" leads to the pilot's groups: making one, which shows its invite code once, with a way to hand it on; joining one with a code; and for each its members and what can be done: the keeper makes a new code (which ends the old), renames the group, removes a member or hands the keeping on by a swipe, and deletes the group; anyone leaves it. ### Flight

The map fills the screen. A panel lies over its lower part, from edge to edge, as the design language's flight screen has it (`design/reference/screens/flight.png`). From top to bottom: the flight in one number, the scored distance in the `hero` style ("104.6 km"), or the track's length until it is scored; what kind of flight it was and the day ("FAI triangle · Sat, Jul 4, 2026"); times and glider; three readings (airtime, highest point, best climb); a card with the altitude profile and the legend for the track colours; and, on the pilot's own flight, the button "Send to a league". On the three apps since 2026-11-20; on the web at desktop width it stands in the column beside the map (see [Web](#web)).

- The track is coloured by climb rate, with a white edge so it reads over any ground. A hollow dot marks the start and a solid dot the end. Under it, a thin dashed line shows the scored route through its turnpoints.
- Dragging along the profile moves a marker along the track, and the readings change to that moment: time, height over the ground or time in the air, altitude, climb. The marker **stays** when the finger lifts, so the pilot can look from the chart to the map. A button in the panel's corner returns to the totals. The play button beside it runs the marker along the whole flight in about half a minute.
- Everything else known about the flight is in a sheet opened from the panel: how the flight scores as a cross-country flight (free flight, free triangle or FAI triangle, with distance and points), the time spent in thermals and on glides, every thermal (when, height gained, average climb, the wind it showed) and every glide (distance, glide ratio, speed), both extremes of altitude and climb, the recorder, the exact meaning of each colour. Choosing a thermal or glide closes the sheet and marks that moment on the map and the profile.
- The map is told how much of it the panel and bars cover, and fits the track into the rest.
- **The flight in the air** (web, since 2026-11-21). "Terrain in 3D" tilts the map over the terrain and lifts the flight to the heights it was flown at, true to the terrain's scale: the track as a ribbon in the climb colours on its dark casing, crossed so that it reads from above and from the side, and a see-through curtain in the primary colour from the track down to the ground under it, where the ground is known (the altitude chart's ground, read from the elevation tiles). The track on the ground goes while it is in the air; the scored route stays on the ground, dashed. Each thermal the core's analysis found stands as a see-through coral column from the height it was entered at to the height it was left at, above the middle of its circles, with its average climb and its gain written at the top ("+2.4 m/s · +591 m"); the glide that lost the most height has its loss written at its middle ("−1161 m on one glide"), in the sink's teal mark. Both numbers are the core's. The marked moment, of the replay and of the chart, is a dark dot at its height with a thin line down to the ground. Two buttons join the map's while it is in 3D: "Whole flight" shows all of the flight from the south-west, leaning back, and "Follow the glider" keeps the camera on the marked moment, looking the way the glider flew; switched off, the camera stays where the pilot put it. Turning, tilting and zooming are the map's own, by mouse, finger and keyboard. Flat, the map is as it was. Not built: airspace as volumes in the air (it lies flat on the terrain, as on the flat map), because the core gives an airspace's floor and ceiling only as words ("1000 ft AGL"), not as heights; the words over the flight are not hidden behind mountains, as the flight itself is. To verify: the view on a real phone, and how a frame performs there (in the test browser, drawing in software, a frame of the layer took about 0.1 to 1 ms for the sample flight).

**What a station says about the air** (in the core and on a station's page in the three apps since 2026-11-21). From what the stations already send, the core gives a station's page three things beside the wind (`station_air`, `station_days`, `station_day_path` and `station_day`). *A cloud base*: the height above sea level at which air lifted from the station would condense, 125 m for every degree between its temperature and its dew point, to the nearest fifty metres, under the words "Estimated cloud base" and with a note that it is an estimate for air rising from the station's height, not a measurement, and no statement that there is cloud. It is absent without a temperature, a humidity or the station's height, for a reading the map shows as old, and while the sun is down at the station, when nothing rises. *The temperature step* to the nearest station that stands at least 500 m higher or lower within 15 km, both read within the hour and within 20 minutes of each other: the other station's name, how far it is and how much higher or lower, the change in °C for every 100 m up, and one of four words for the air between them, "Warmer above: an inversion", "Hardly cooler above" (less than 0.4 °C), "Cooler above, as in air at rest" (0.4 °C to under 0.8 °C) and "Much cooler above: air that turns over" (0.8 °C and more), with the limits the word stands for in a sentence and a note that two stations on the ground are not a sounding and that it says nothing about whether one can fly. The limits are ours and the owner's to decide. *Past days*: one day of the station's readings chosen by the pilot, from a year back to today, for the chart the last 24 hours have; a day without readings says so in words. The numbers are the core's values, set by each app as it sets the others; the words and the notes are the core's. Where the platforms differ: the day is chosen with the browser's date field on the web, the system's compact date picker on the iPhone and Material's date picker on Android, each held to the days the core names, and choosing today goes back to the last 24 hours on all three. *Not seen yet:* a cloud base on any screen, because the development server's clock stands after dark, and a past day's chart with readings on the phones, because that server has readings of its own day alone (the web's test moves a day's readings back and says that they are made up).

- **One line of time for all layers** (`RadarView.moments`, `first_moment`; in the core and on the three apps' maps since 2026-11-21). The view has the stops of the card's strip, the oldest first, and the one the card opens at, the newest. Each stop (`RadarMoment`) has its time on the pilot's clock ("14:15"), the line to write on the map, and the pictures to draw, bottom first, each named by its layer and its frame (`RadarMomentFrame`). A layer's picture at a stop is its newest frame whose interval had ended by then; a layer has none there if it has no such frame, or if that frame was by then older than three of the layer's own intervals, the rule by which a layer is `stale`. The line is the topmost picture's own, and says of a picture under it that is of another moment: "Rain radar, 14:15, 4 minutes ago. Austria's analysis is of 14:00." **How an app draws a moment:** for each of the moment's frames, bottom first, it takes the layer's picture at that frame's `path`, has the core colour it with a fully clear colour for "not seen" unless the frame's `draws_unseen` is true, and pins it by that layer's four corners. The colouring is `radar_frame_image_under`, which gets the picture of the frame at place i of the moment's `frames` and, as `above`, the pictures of the frames after it (i + 1 to the last; none for the topmost) as the server sent them, and gives the picture back clear wherever one above it sees, rain or dry: the two see-through pictures never add up to a colour the key does not have, and the older analysis never shows rain where the radar has just measured none. `draws_unseen` is true for the lowest picture of the moment, which is the layer's own `draws_unseen` unless that layer has no picture at the moment; so a place is veiled where no picture of the moment sees it, and never shown as dry. An app keeps no frame of its own choosing on the map: a layer without a picture at the moment is not drawn at it. With one layer the stops are that layer's frames, one for one, with the frames' own lines. For the caution, each layer still says for itself whether it is behind (`stale`, `stale_words`), and a card with one line for it shows that of the topmost layer that has frames.
- **Old rain is not the rain of now.** When the newest frame is older than three of its source's intervals, a quarter of an hour for DWD and three quarters for the analysis, each layer by its own, the layer is `stale` and says so in words for the place the label stands in: "The newest radar picture is 25 minutes old. It is not the rain of now." A layer without frames says "No radar picture yet". The ages are counted against the device's clock, and against the server's where the device's is behind it.

The web and the iPhone show them since 2026-11-21 (the iPhone's rows stand at the foot of its table, its line in the table's footnote, in the web's words), where the model has an estimate for some hour of the day: "Thermals, climb" in m/s and "Thermals, top" in metres, with "base" in small print under a top that is the cloud base, and a line under the table that they are an estimate worked out from the model's heat and temperatures, not a value of the model; the table still says nothing about whether the day can be flown.

**Airspace at a place, and by height** (in the core and in the three apps since 2026-11-21). Asked at a place on the map, the core lists what lies over it (`AirspaceSet::over_place`): every airspace whose outline contains the place, from the lowest floor up, each with its name, what it is, floor and ceiling as published, how Venti treats it ("Avoid", "Caution", "Free") and why, what its source publishes about its use (the marks such as "Active by NOTAM", and hours by day of the week, as published and not evaluated), and where the data is from and of what date. A line at the head says how many there are, or that there is none, or that the app has no airspace data for the place, which is not the same; under the list always stands that the data is not certified and no clearance, and that nothing says what is active today. For the map's airspace layer the core has a filter by height (`airspace_height_filter`, `AirspaceSet::in_area_below`): all heights, or up to 4500 m, 3500 m or 2500 m, which takes airspace that certainly begins above the chosen height off the map and changes nothing else, and says so. The rules are in [airspace.md](airspace.md#what-lies-over-a-place-and-the-filter-by-height). The gesture and the place of the filter, decided on 2026-11-21 for the apps to build and the owner to see: a tap (on the web a click) on the map where airspace is drawn and nothing else of Venti's lies under the finger opens the list as a sheet named by the place. Something else under the finger wins: a station, a site, a glider, and while the "Forecast" layer is on a square of it, whose sheet a tap opens there. A long press stays the forecast's. The filter by height stands at the foot of that sheet as the pack's segmented control, under the core's title ("Airspace on the map, up to") and in the core's words ("All", "4500 m", "3500 m", "2500 m": short, because four longer ones touched each other on a phone), with the core's note under it; the choice is kept like the aerial view's and holds for every map of the app that draws airspace except the cockpit's, which always draws all of it. As built: the sheet opens from the map tab; the filter holds there, on a flight's page and on another pilot's flight (the route planner draws no airspace on the phones). The web and Android open the sheet wherever the core lists airspace over the place, also inside one that is drawn by its outline alone; the iPhone opens it only where the list holds airspace the map is drawing, so not for airspace the filter has taken off. *Open, for the owner:* how "Free" is marked. The web and Android give it the pack's good status, green, as they give "Avoid" the danger status and "Caution" the caution status; the iPhone writes it in plain words, so that green is not read as "safe" over data that is not certified. One of the two should hold on all three.

### The page of a shared flight

A flight that its pilot shares has a page on the web, made by the server, which works without the web app and without scripts. It is the first thing someone outside the app sees of Venti, usually as a preview in a chat.

- The page follows the flight screen in what it puts first: a headline that says what the flight was ("42.3 km closed FAI triangle"), who flew it and when; then the track's shape; then four numbers set large (airtime, distance, points, highest point); then the altitude profile; then everything else as a plain list; then one button, to download the flight file.
- The track is a line in the accent colour on a faintly tinted panel, north up, with a hollow dot at the start and a solid dot at the end, as in the app. There is no map under it, because there is no tile server yet.
- It follows light and dark from the visitor's system, uses the system's own typeface, and sets numbers in the rounded face with fixed-width digits where the system has one.
- The drawings say in words what they show, for those who do not see them, and the altitude profile's lowest and highest values stand in its caption rather than as tiny labels inside it.
- The preview picture (1200 by 630) shows the same track on the same panel and three numbers beside it: distance with what it was scored as, airtime, points. It is always light, because a picture cannot follow the reader's appearance. Its numbers are set in Nunito, the open typeface closest to the rounded system face. A pilot's name is not on it: the link's title carries it.
- Points are called "scored by Venti" on the picture and explained in the page's footer: they follow the World XContest's rules as we read them and are not the league's result.

To verify: how the preview looks in the chat apps pilots use (WhatsApp, Signal, Telegram, iMessage). It was built to the Open Graph and Twitter-card conventions and has been looked at in a browser only. The names of the Open Graph lines were checked against <https://ogp.me/> on 2026-11-19. The names of the `twitter:` lines were not checked against X's own documentation, whose page for them could not be reached that day; they are the ones in common use.

**The ground under a flight.** A flight's altitude profile is drawn over the ground it was flown over: the earth as a filled shape along the foot of the chart, the flight's line above it, and the room between them the height over the ground. With the marker on the track, the panel reads that height in place of the time in the air. The ground is read at 240 moments of the flight from the map's elevation tiles under it, fetched when the flight is opened (a few megabytes for a flight of some tens of kilometres, coarser tiles for a longer one) and kept on the iPhone; until they are there, and where there are none, the chart is as it was. The same holds for another pilot's flight. Built on the iPhone on 2026-11-20, on the web the same day (what differs there is under [Web](#web)), and on Android on 2026-11-21 as the iPhone has it, with one difference: when a tile gets no answer the tiles after it are not asked for, as Android's cockpit already does it. *Open, for the owner:* the colour of the earth is no token of the pack. The iPhone and Android write the same value out in one named place each (`groundColor`, `GroundColour`), the web has `--ground`, the same in both appearances; all three wait for a token.

**Two flights side by side.** A flight's details have "Lay another flight beside it". The pilot chooses one: what other pilots show to everyone and flew around the same place within twelve hours ("Flown here that day"), or one of their own, those nearest in time first. The second flight is drawn on the map under the first, as a line of one colour, a green that no climb band and nothing else on the page has; its altitude is a second, thinner line in the profile; and a line in the panel names whose it is. Two flights that were in the air at the same time for a minute or more are laid over each other by the clock: dragging along the profile moves both gliders, each to where it was at that moment, and the line then says how high the other was and how far away ("1,840 m · 320 m away", or "Not in the air then"). Flights that were not in the air together are each laid out from their first moment, and the line says so ("Flown 3 days apart. Each is laid out from its first moment."). A tap on the line opens "Side by side": the two in numbers, this flight and the other in columns (airtime, scored distance, the length of the track, the highest point, the best climb, and from the thermals and glides the climb in thermals, the height gained, the glide and the share of the time spent circling), the number that is ahead in bold, never both. For flights flown together it has "Go to where they were nearest", which puts the marker on that moment. "Take the other flight away" ends it. The map makes room for both tracks, and the profile for both in height; where the second flight begins before the first or ends after it, its line is cut at the edges. Nothing of another pilot's flight is kept on the iPhone. Built on the iPhone on 2026-11-20, and on Android the same day; the development mode has no two flights in the air together, so that case is tested with the sample flight moved in time.

**Height over the ground.** Under the altitude the cockpit says how high the glider is over the ground, in place of the height over the take-off, wherever the iPhone has the ground: the elevation tiles the map draws its relief from, fetched for the five by five tiles around the glider (some thirteen kilometres every way) every ten seconds if any are missing, and kept on the iPhone, so that the ground of a place flown at before is there without a network. Where it has none the line says the height over the take-off as before. Nothing warns of the ground: the number is there to be read. The ground does change what the cockpit says of the places it could glide to: where the straight line to a place meets a ridge first, the line under the map says "Ground in the way" with how far off, in place of the arrival height, the list of places shows a mountain in red beside it, and the landing named over the map is one with nothing in the way.

**The wind on the ground.** In flight a pilot knows the wind at their own height, from the drift of their circles; what it does in the valley, where they will land, the wind stations know. With a Venti server set, the cockpit asks it every two minutes in flight for the stations around the glider and around the place the pilot means to land: the place the cockpit navigates to, unless that is the take-off, else the landing it names as within reach. A badge under the wind's shows the wind of one station, with a flag: mean and gusts in km/h and an arrow where it blows; the station that speaks for the landing if there is one, else the nearest. A station that neither speaks for the landing nor stands within 15 km of the glider has its distance beside its wind ("1/5 · 54 km"), because a wind from that far is another valley's. A tap opens the wind's sheet, which then begins with its second part, "On the ground": the nearest stations within 75 km with a reading of the last hour, each with its name, how far and which way it stands and how high, what it measured and how long ago. The radius is wide because the stations of the weather services stand tens of kilometres apart, and a summit station a valley away is what shows a foehn; some of their readings come three quarters of an hour late, which is why the age stands beside each. A station speaks for a landing when it stands within 3 km of it and within 250 m of its height. When its wind is strong, an orange band lies over the map, "Strong wind at Sample landing field", with the station, its distance from the landing, the wind and its age; it is said aloud once, after anything about airspace, and the cross puts it away until another station warns or this one's wind has dropped and risen again. Strong is measured against the glider: a mean wind that leaves less than 10 km/h of way into it at the pilot's trim speed, or gusts as fast as the glider; without a trim speed, 25 km/h or gusts of 35. Without a network, or while Venti is not reached, the cockpit says nothing about the ground's wind, and no word is not a word that it is calm. To verify in the air and with pilots: the 3 km and 250 m, what counts as strong, and whether the badge earns its place. With the stations the server has today (the two weather services') few landings have a station that close; the warning waits for denser sources.

**Others in the air.** In flight the cockpit's map shows the gliders of pilots who show their flight to everyone, some thirty kilometres around: the same small wings as on the map tab, turned the way they fly, with name and altitude, green while they fly and grey when nothing was heard of them for a while. They are asked for every fifteen seconds from the server set in the settings, which needs a network in the air; without an answer the wings stay where they were. The pilot's own glider, if the flight is shown to everyone, is not drawn a second time. A glider cannot be tapped in the cockpit: who it is and how old its position is, is on the map tab. Flights shared with a link only are not there, and there is no way yet to show a flight to a group alone.

**How far it glides.** Where the iPhone has the ground, the cockpit's map has a green line around the glider: the edge of what a straight glide still reaches with a hundred metres to spare, every five degrees around, by the glider's own glide in the wind as last worked out, over the ground as it is. Over a flat valley it is a wide ring pushed downwind; towards a mountain it lies on the slope, close by. What lies behind a ridge is outside the line, although a pilot may get there another way: the line shows what is certain by a straight glide, not everything that is possible. It is worked out anew every two seconds. Lower than a hundred metres over the ground there is no line. Where the line lies at the end of the ground the iPhone holds and not at the end of the glide, it is drawn the same; the glider then reaches at least that far. To verify in the air: the reserve, and whether the line helps or clutters.

**Ahead, from the side.** With airspace to warn from, or the ground to draw, the cockpit's map has a second round button under the one for the whole flight. It opens the side view: height against distance for the next ten kilometres, each airspace on the line a block in its colour from its floor to its ceiling with its name, the glider a dot at the left, and its line running from it, dashed. On a glide the view looks along the course and the line is the glide of the last seconds; while circling a course says nothing, so it looks to where the cockpit navigates and draws the glider's own glide in the wind as last worked out. Above the picture stands what matters most in a line ("CTR Innsbruck in 1.9 km, on your line", "… 150 m below your line", "No airspace to avoid on this line for 10 km"), and under it each airspace with its limits as published, how far it is, and whether the line runs through it. Where the app holds the ground (the map's elevation tiles around the glider), the picture has it: the ground as a filled shape along the foot, a second line of words that says where the glide meets it ("Your line meets the ground in 4.2 km") or by how much it stays clear at the least, and airspace given above ground standing on it. Beyond where the line meets the ground an airspace is named and not measured against the line. The ground is a model of the bare ground in pixels 13 m wide: it knows no trees, cables or buildings, and the picture says so. Where the app does not hold the ground, a limit given above ground is drawn as far as it may reach, fainter, and said to be uncertain; the foot of the picture is then not the ground, and the line is not cut off where the ground would be. Airspace a paraglider needs nothing for is left out. It is a picture for planning a glide; the warnings, with their margins and their sound, are the banner's. Built on the iPhone on 2026-11-20 and tried against a replayed flight; not flown.

**A map without a network.** A Venti server hands out the map of a region as two files, the base map and the elevation; the iPhone downloads them and draws from them. The page is "Offline maps", reached from the Fly tab's list of what to check before take-off ("Offline map", with "None" or the region's name beside it) and from the settings, under the server. It lists the regions on the iPhone first, each with a tick and "On this device, 412 MB", then what else the server has, each with its size. "Download" asks first, naming the size and that mobile data counts. While it runs the row has a bar, "123 MB of 412 MB", and "Stop the download". A download that breaks off keeps what has arrived and says so in red; "Go on with the download" asks the server for the rest only. When everything is there the files are checked against the size and the SHA-256 the server gave, and a file that is not the server's is thrown away, with words that say so. A region of which the server has a newer edition says "A newer map can be downloaded" and keeps working until the newer one is whole. "Delete from this iPhone" asks first. Under the list the page says what a download does and names whose data the kept regions hold, with links.

What is drawn from where: the cockpit's map is drawn from a kept region wherever the glider is inside one, with a network or without, so that the map checked on the ground is the map there is in the air; an arrow in a circle on the cockpit's status line says so. Outside every kept region the cockpit draws from the network as before. The map tab and a flight's map come from the network while the iPhone has one, since that reaches everywhere, and from a kept region when it has none. While the map tab draws a kept region, its credits list that region's own in place of the base map's line, on both phones since 2026-11-22; not yet seen on a screen. The height of the ground is read from the region's elevation file wherever it reaches, before anything is fetched. Not built: downloads that carry on while Venti is in the background, a choice of which map to prefer, and a word before take-off when the place flown at lies outside every kept region. Built on 2026-11-20 against a server played by the tests and against the development mode, which hands out the small region that comes with the app. No real region exists yet, and nothing of this has run on an iPhone.

**A flight once more, in the cockpit.** The menu of a flight's page has "Replay in the cockpit", in real time or ten or twenty times faster. The flight is played through the same cockpit a pilot flies with, from its own file: the wind it works out from the circles, the picture of each thermal, the airspace warnings, a task if one is kept. Nothing is recorded and nothing is shared. On the iPhone and on Android the word "Replay" at the top of the cockpit is a menu: the replay can be held at a moment, to look at what the cockpit shows there, and run on in real time or ten or twenty times faster. A replay does not leap: when the phone was busy with something else for more than two seconds, the replay carries on from where the flight was instead of jumping over minutes of it that nobody saw. It is how a pilot sees what the cockpit would have said on a day they remember, and how the cockpit's rules are tried against real flights before anyone trusts them in the air.

### Live tracking in the app

Sharing a flight while it is under way is a choice between three audiences, on one page that is reached from the Fly tab before take-off ("Live tracking: Off") and from a round button in the cockpit: **Nobody**, **People I give the link**, **Everyone**. The choice is kept for the next flights, which is why the Fly tab shows it before every start.

### The page of a live track

A pilot who shares a live track sends a link. Whoever opens it, someone at home or the driver who comes to fetch the pilot, has no app and no account, and may be worried. The page is made by the server for that reader, with no script, and reads from top to bottom as the answer to "is everything all right, and where?".

- **The headline is the state, in words:** "Dario B. is under way", "Dario B. has landed", or "Dario B. was last heard of 40 minutes ago". Under it one sentence says what is known: whether a landing was reported, and how old the last position is.
- **Silence is never a landing, and never an alarm.** A track without a position for more than half an hour says exactly that, and adds that this is what a phone without a network or with an empty battery looks like, and that it does not say what happened. "Landed" stands on the page only when the pilot's app said so.
- **Then four numbers, set large** as on the flight page: altitude, the age of the position, speed over the ground, climb. The last two only where the app sent them.
- **Then the line of the track**, the last half hour of it at most, north up on the tinted panel of the flight page, with a hollow dot where the line begins and a larger filled dot at the newest position. There is no map under it. Its caption says in words what it shows.

### Climb and sink

A track is coloured in five bands of climb rate, averaged over ten seconds: two blues for sink, grey for level flight, orange and red for climb. Warm means up.

| Band | Rate (m/s) | Colour |
|---|---|---|
| Strong sink | below −2.0 | `#184F95` |
| Sink | −2.0 to −0.5 | `#3987E5` |
| Level | −0.5 to +0.5 | `#85847F` |
| Climb | +0.5 to +2.0 | `#EB6834` |
| Strong climb | +2.0 and above | `#B8242B` |

A glider's own sink rate in still air is a little over 1 m/s, so an ordinary glide is "sink" blue and thermals stand out against it in orange and red.

The five colours were checked together on 2026-11-18 with a palette validator: every pair stays apart for red-green colour blindness (worst pair 9.7 on the OKLab scale, target 8), for normal vision (16.8, floor 15), and each has at least 3:1 contrast against a light surface. Blue against orange is the pairing that survives colour blindness; red against green does not, and green is reserved for "flyable".

To verify: no flying app we read documents which colours it uses for climb and sink, so "warm means up" is our choice, taken from weather maps and gliding instruments. Check it with pilots.

### Wind

In lists and tables on a dark surface the pack's dark scale runs the other way, dim to bright, so that strong wind stands out there too. The map keeps the light scale in both appearances: its arrows have an edge in the map's halo and the map itself stays light. The pack's own breaks every 5 km/h wait for the core to sort by them.

| Band | Mean wind (km/h) | The pack's step | Light | Dark |
|---|---|---|---|---|

### Flyable, marginal, not flyable

The design language's Today screen says of a take-off whether the day is for flying: a status of icon, word and colour ("Flyable", "Marginal", "Not flyable"), the day in a line ("Good from noon") and what limits it ("Gusts to 31 km/h after 15:00"). The words are made in the core (`core::today`, `site_outlook`) from the forecast of the take-off's place and what the site's source says of it, the wind directions it works in and its height. It never says "safe" or "go", and it names the model, its run and its grid cell: it is a reading of a weather model for a cell kilometres wide, and knows nothing of the take-off itself.

Each daylight hour, from an hour after sunrise to an hour before sunset, is judged by the worst thing the finest model has against it:

| What the model says | Marginal from | Not flyable from |
|---|---|---|
| Rain or snow in the hour | | 0.2 mm |
| Wind 10 m above the model's ground | 20 km/h | 30 km/h |
| Gusts | 30 km/h | 40 km/h |
| Wind from a direction the take-off does not work in | 8 km/h | 15 km/h |
| Wind at the model's levels up to 1,500 m above the take-off | 35 km/h | 50 km/h |
| Base of convective cloud | less than 200 m above the take-off | below the take-off |
| CAPE | 800 J/kg ("Showers or thunderstorms can grow") | |

**A day is one model's**, never a blend of two: the finest model the server has for the place, unless a coarser one has more of the day's hours. So today and tomorrow are ICON-D2's, and the day its run ends in, or a day after that, is ICON-EU's where ICON-EU reaches it; each day carries its model, that model's run and its ground, and the foot of the screen names the model of the day that is shown.

A day is flyable when two hours in a row are, and its line says when: "Good all day", "Good from noon", "Good until 15:00", "Good from 11:00 to 15:00", with what ends or delays the stretch beside it. It is marginal when two hours in a row are at least marginal, with the reason met most often. **A day the model's run ends in**, with daylight left after its last hour, is judged by the hours there are and says where they end (the core, since 2026-11-21): a good stretch that lasts to the run's last hour is "Good until 14:00" with "The forecast ends at 14:00" beside it, not "Good all day", and the foot of the screen adds "The model's run ends at 14:00 on this day: the hours after are not judged." That is mostly the last day of a forecast: ICON-EU is asked for the third day after today, and its runs end on it at 06, 12 or 18 UTC, or with it ([data-sources.md](data-sources.md#what-a-series-holds)). A take-off whose source names no directions is not judged by the direction, one without a height not by the cloud or the wind higher up. What the day's thermals are worth is a reading of its own, beside this one: [Thermals](#thermals).

The limits are our own, round numbers a cautious pilot would nod to, the same for every pilot and every wing. To verify with pilots, all of them; the owner decides them.

### Thermals

Asked for by the owner on 2026-11-21: "The most useful thing about the burnair app is the thermal forecast. And they do it using the ICON D model, and they can tell if it's going to be a super day with color blue, or if it's going to be a day where you can do cross-country but not record flying, or it's a day where you can just fly safely and not die, or if it's a day where you should not fly at all."

Burnair's method is not open ([data-sources.md](data-sources.md#forecasts)). Venti's is its own, made in the core from what ICON-D2 publishes for the grid cell of a take-off, hour by hour (`venti_flight::convection`), and it is an estimate of the air over that cell, never a promise of a climb:

- **How high thermals reach.** Air that leaves the ground at the ground's temperature cools by 9.8 °C for every kilometre it rises, and rises for as long as it is warmer than the air around it, which the model gives at its levels. Where it no longer is, thermals end: the parcel method of a sounding diagram.
- **Whether cloud caps them.** Where the model itself has convective cloud, its base is the ceiling. Where it has none, rising air is taken to condense 125 m above the ground for every degree between temperature and dew point there; if that lies below where the thermals end, there is cumulus at that height, and if not the day is blue.
- **How strong they are.** The convective velocity scale, `w* = (g / T · H / (ρ · cp) · depth)^(1/3)`: the more heat `H` the ground gives to the air and the deeper the layer the thermals stir, the faster the air in them rises. It is what RASP's forecasts call the thermal updraft velocity. A paraglider is taken to climb by that less 1.0 m/s of its own sink.

The heat is the model's own balance of sun, cloud, snow and wet ground, so a closed sky, a snow cover and a morning are in the figure without a rule for each.

**What is weak in it, all to verify against climbs that were flown:**

- The model's levels are far apart: 80, 120 and 180 m above its ground, then the pressure surfaces at about 1,500 m, 3,000 m and 4,200 m above the sea. An inversion between two of them is seen only as a weaker average, so the height can be several hundred metres off.
- The velocity scale was found over flat land. In the mountains sunlit slopes gather the heat, and climbs are often stronger than it says and weaker in a lee.
- The model's ground is smoothed. A take-off above it is judged by how far the thermals reach above the take-off, which is right for the height and says nothing of the slope the take-off stands on.
- The glider's sink of 1.0 m/s and every limit in the table are our own round numbers; the owner decides them.
- The pilot's own assistant gets the same figures: its `forecast_at` tool gives each hour's `thermal_climb_ms` and `thermal_top_m` with a caveat that says they are an estimate and how it is made. It gives no kind of day: the limits for that are read at a take-off's height, which the tool does not know.
- Nothing is known yet of how the estimate compares with flights. The server holds flown tracks with their thermals and could set each against the forecast of its hour and place: a way to check the limits, not built.

A shell asks at `foehn_path()` and hands the answer to `foehn(status, body, places, now)` with the places of the pilot's sites and Venti's clock (0 while it is not read, never the device's); it gets a `FoehnView` and writes no word of its own.

**What is built**, on the three apps, as the proposal below has it except where this says otherwise:

- *The line on Today*, over the take-offs, only when the core gives one, with the caution sign and the status colour of its level (on Android and the iPhone the danger's colour for "likely", not the warning's); a tap opens the view.
- *A row at Today's foot*, "Foehn" (the view's title from the core, the app's own word while Venti gave no answer), there whether or not there is a line, so that the view can be opened on a day the pressure says nothing. On the web it is there when the core gave a view or a fault; on Android once Today has loaded or failed; on the iPhone it is the page's last row (`today-foehn-row`).
- *The view* as a sheet: each pair with its title, badge, words, `when`, its last 24 hours as a chart with zero in the middle and the limits as lines, and its note in small print; then the explanation, the caution and the credits. On Android a fault has "Try again". The iPhone asks when Today has its take-offs and again on a pull, and reads a held answer again for ten minutes without asking.
- *Not built:* the link from the map's layers sheet; nothing of foehn is on the map.
- **The pressures are the providers' own** (since `a74f15d9`). Austria's stations now come with GeoSphere's own reduced pressure (`PRED`, taken where it is a pressure at sea level, from 950 hPa up), as Bolzano's comes with South Tyrol's, so all four stations of the two pairs are reduced by their providers. Venti's own reduction from a station's listed height was 3.7 hPa low at Innsbruck on 2026-11-22, whose barometer hangs above the height it is listed at: on the real stations of that morning Bolzano–Innsbruck read "possible" at 3.0 hPa, where the first version had said "likely" at 6.7 ([architecture.md](architecture.md#decision-log), [data-sources.md](data-sources.md#geospheres-pressures)).
- **Two kinds of pair keep their row in the view and never speak on Today.** A pair that is Venti's own guess (the core, `8cab5250`): one whose stations or limits no weather service gave, marked so (`to_verify` in the server's answer), which is Klagenfurt and Salzburg. And a pair with a pressure Venti reduced to sea level itself (the core, `a74f15d9`), because its difference is not sure; that is no pair of the two today, and any pair whenever a provider sends only the pressure at the station. So only Bolzano and Innsbruck can put a line on Today.
- **The notes say which limits no weather service gave.** Bolzano and Innsbruck's note: the pair and the limits of 3 and 6 hPa are the foehn chart of the Province of Bolzano's weather service, given for north foehn, and Venti uses the same limits for south foehn, which no weather service has given. Klagenfurt and Salzburg's: Venti's own choice of airfields with the first pair's limits, given by no weather service. Where a station sent only its pressure at its own height and Venti reduced it, the note names the station, says that the number can be off by several hectopascals and that the pair gives Today no line.
- *Tested:* Android by two UI tests (light and dark), the web by a browser test (`e2e/foehn.spec.ts`) and the iPhone by a UI test (`testTodaySaysWhenFoehnIsLikelyAndShowsThePressures`) and the model's unit tests, all against the development server's made-up pressures; that their screenshots were looked at is to verify, and no app has seen a real day of foehn.

**The proposal for the screens,** as it was written before they were built:

- **Today: one line, and only when it has something to say.** `FoehnView.today_line` is there when a pair within 150 km of one of the pilot's sites is at "possible" or "likely": "South foehn likely: 6 hPa higher at Bolzano than at Innsbruck", in the caution's colour for `Possible` and the warning's for `Likely`, above the sites. Without it Today shows nothing in its place: no line is not "no foehn". A tap opens the view. It changes no site's verdict.
- **The view, opened on purpose** from that line (and from the map's layer sheet as a link, not a layer): the title; each `FoehnRow` with its title ("Bolzano – Innsbruck"), its words, its badge (`level_label`, coloured by `level`; `Unknown` greyed, `None` plain and never green), `when`, and a small chart of `points` over `chart_title`, zero in the middle, `above_label` and `below_label` at its two sides, the `limits` as four thin lines; the row's `note` as small print; then `explanation`, `caution` in full, and the `credits`. `empty` when there is no row.
- **Nothing on the map by default,** and no new control on a default screen. Lines between the stations, as Burnair draws them, would be a layer that is off until asked for; not proposed now.

Against an unknown clock the words carry the reading's own time ("South foehn likely at 18:50 UTC: …") and `when` is that time.

## Web

The web app is the same product in a browser: the same screens, the same words. It has the Map with live wind, flying sites, the forecast for a place and who is in the air, the Logbook with everyone's flights beside the pilot's own, the Plans of the pilot's groups for a flying day and, under Settings, the pilot's account and airspace; the cockpit and its settings are for the phone. A browser has no system components to borrow, so the app draws its own, as plainly as it can, and keeps to what a browser does have: its dialogs, its popovers, its focus ring, the reader's text size, the system's light or dark appearance.

**What it wears** (since 2026-11-20): the design language. `src/app.css` imports the pack's `design/tokens/tokens.css`, which brings Fira Sans from `design/fonts` (served by the app; `font-src 'self'`), and maps the app's own names onto the tokens: paper and indigo surfaces, the pack's text colours, its border for hairlines and flat cards, its primary for a chosen control and a main button, its link colour for text that leads somewhere, its focus colour for the focus ring, its radii, its 48 px target and its type styles in rem. Light and dark follow the system through the pack's own themes; the `flight` theme is not used, a browser having no cockpit. What is drawn on the map as values (tracks, wind arrows, gliders, dots) takes the pack's light values from `design/tokens/tokens.json` (`src/lib/design/palette.ts`): a track in the seven steps of the vario scale, teal for sink to coral and red for lift, with a dark casing; the wind in four steps of the pack's purple wind scale, the step that holds the middle of each of the core's four bands; a flying glider in the pack's "good" green. The flying icons where the pack has one (map, wind, caution, wing, site, track for the logbook, league) come from `design/assets/icons`; the general ones (back, close, plus, menu and the like) stay the app's own at the pack's 2 px stroke, because the pack leaves those to Lucide, which is not a dependency of the app. The favicons, the touch icon and the manifest are the pack's (`design/assets/app-icon/web`), written into the site by the build; the navigation's rail shows the pack's small app icon. The navigation is the pack's tab bar along the bottom of a narrow screen, solid with a hairline, the current section's name in the text colour and its icon in the pack's coral text colour; the sections are Home, Map, Today, Logbook, Plans and Settings (by the web's notes; the pack's tab names being placeholders by its own word). No name is cut short: at 360 px with text at 130 % "Logbook" and "Settings" break at a soft hyphen onto a second line under the icon and the bar grows with them, the plain name staying the link's accessible name (since the last round of 2026-11-22). Segmented controls are the pack's: a pill, the chosen one filled in the primary colour. Nothing floats on glass any more: what lies over a map is a card with the pack's shadow.

**Not yet in the design language on the web, and why:** the base map has the pack's light map colours in the dark appearance too, the pack's dark map waiting for a second style; airspace keeps the colours [airspace.md](airspace.md) gives, the pack's being proposals; the valley art and the spiral loader are not used, a web page of data having no arrival moment that needs them; there is no coral button, the web having no action that starts or records a flight; the status chips (icon, word, colour) wait for a status the app shows, flyability not being built.

**On a large screen the layout is denser** (the owner, 2026-11-21): "I do appreciate your mobile design, but it's too much. On desktop, I want to have a very compact and information-dense design. Then as the viewport gets smaller, it should be more mobile app-like. I hate desktop websites that look like an app. So I'm not saying to go super tiny like XContest, but macOS drives a lot of my inspiration. The UI of macOS is very different than the UI of iOS, and I respect the choices." A first reading of this made the wide layout an imitation of a Mac application, and the owner corrected it the same day: "I didn't say that the web should act like a macOS desktop app. I just said it should have a more information-dense layout when you are on a device with a larger screen. Didn't say to copy a MacBook, please." So the website stays a website in Venti's own look, and what changes with the width is how much is on the screen:

- **More at once.** Where the narrow layout stacks things or opens them one after another, the wide one sets them side by side: a list and beside it what the chosen row is, a flight's map beside its figures and charts. Choosing a row fills what is beside it; it does not leave the screen.
- **Tables where there are rows.** Flights, pilots, wind stations, sites, airspaces and plans are tables with columns, headings that sort, rows about 32 px high, and the numbers in columns that line up. More of what the core already knows is on screen at once.
- **Compact controls and type.** The pack's label style (14 px) is the body's size and its caption (12 px) the secondary one; controls are about 32 px high, spacing from the lower steps of the pack's scale. The 48 px touch target, the large cards, the bottom sheets and the tab bar belong to the narrow layout.
- **Nothing of a Mac is copied.** No window toolbar, no sidebar made to look like a file browser's, no menu on a right click, no keys of a desktop program. The shapes, colours, type and icons are the pack's, as on a phone. What any good website does stays: the pointer over a thing shows that it can be clicked, and everything can be reached and worked with the keyboard.
- **Narrow turns into the app.** Below the width at which things no longer fit side by side, the same screens are the phone's: one column, the tab bar, cards, sheets, large targets. In between, what stands beside the list drops out first and becomes a screen of its own.
- Not as small as XContest: nothing under the pack's caption size, and the map screens keep their map.

The wide layout described below was built before this and stretches the phone more than it should; it is to be rebuilt to the above, screen by screen, the logbook and a flight first.

Where everyone's flights differ on the web from the iPhone's:

- **Three pages under the Logbook.** "Mine", "Everyone" and "Ranking" stand under the title as links drawn as one switch, where the site has a Venti server; a site without one has no switch. Everyone's flights have an address of their own, and so have a pilot's flights, each flight and the ranking of each year.

Three flights and the calendar year are what was built for there to be a ranking to look at: the owner has not decided the rule.

- **What the core gives for a search, a month and a pilot's totals** (the server and the core since 2026-11-21; on all three apps since 2026-11-22: a search field over everyone's flights whose results open the pilot's page, the months of the chosen year as chips under the ranking's years where pressing the chosen month again gives the year back, and on a pilot's page the season choice, the core's rows, the take-offs flown from most, the twelve months as bars, the core's note and the flights' terms; on a wide web page the totals are the side column. Choosing a season does not narrow the list of flights under it: the core has no list of one year). *A search for pilots:* `pilots_search_path` asks for the pilots whose handle or name holds what was typed, from two letters on, and `pilots_found` reads a line for each, "Lena Probe (@lena), 3 public flights", with the handle to list that pilot's flights by (`pilot=` of everyone's flights) and one line for the result ("2 pilots", or that nobody by this name has shared a flight). Days on which nobody flew say so: "No public flights on these days".

The app itself stores none: a flight opened without a network has the ground only if the browser still has its tiles. Leaving the flight stops the fetching.
- **The flight's line lies over the ground**, not under it as on the iPhone: where a recorder's altitude runs a few metres under the model's ground, at a take-off or a landing, the line stays in sight.
- **Not drawn across a gap.** Where a tile could not be had, the ground is left out for that stretch of the flight instead of being drawn straight across it, and the marker there reads the time in the air as before.
- **In words for a screen reader.** Unmarked, the chart says between which heights the flight and the ground under it lie ("From 786 to 2,117 metres, over ground from 731 to 1,710 metres."); with the marker set it reads the height over the ground between the altitude and the climb.

Where reporting differs on the web from the iPhone's:

- **Where it is reached.** "Report this flight" is the last entry of another pilot's flight's menu. "Report this pilot" is a button under a pilot's flights, which have no menu. "Report this track" is the last row of a glider's page on the map.
- **One server.** The account and what is reported are on the server the site is served from, so the form has no "signed in on another server" to say. Signed out, it says that a report is sent from an account, and has a link to the account's screen and nothing to fill in.
- **A sheet with a button.** The form is a sheet over the page, titled by what is reported, with "Send report" at its foot, disabled until a reason is chosen. The reasons are radio buttons with a tick at the end of the chosen row, as where a flight's audience is chosen.
- **A refusal goes when the pilot changes the report.** What the core or the server said about the report as it was ("Say in a few words what is wrong.") is taken away when the reason or the words change. Once the words are more than a report has room for, their number stands under the field in red.
- **Afterwards** the sheet holds the core's one line and nothing else. Opened again, the form is empty.

The flight's panel on the web, since 2026-11-20, is the design pack's flight screen as the iPhone has it: the flight in one number in the `hero` style (the scored distance, or the track's length until it is scored), under it what kind of flight it was and the day ("Free flight, Sat, 4 Jul 2026"), then the times and the glider; three readings in the `instrument-m` size (airtime, highest, best climb), which become the four of a marked moment as before; the altitude profile and the climb legend on a card of their own; and on the pilot's own flight a primary button "Send to a league", which is no longer in the menu. On a wide screen all of it stands at the head of the column beside the map, with the details under it.

Where "Send to a league" differs on the web from the iPhone's:

- **XContest only.** The sheet has "Before it goes" (the deadline and the signature, each with a sign, a word in bold and the core's sentence; late and unsigned in the pack's caution colour) and "XContest" ("Save the flight file", which downloads the file as it was imported, and "Open XContest's claim page" in a new tab), with the leagues' rules as links. Under "DHV-XC" one paragraph says that the web app does not send there: a flight goes to a league from the pilot's own device and never through Venti, and whether the DHV-XC lets a web page send to it is not known (*to verify*: the DHV-XC's answer to a request from another origin); the pilot sends it from Venti on a phone or uploads the file on the DHV-XC's pages. No upload password is ever asked for in a browser.
- **The deadline is counted by the browser's clock**, as every time on the web.

Where the web's account and sharing differ from the iPhone's:

- **No server address.** The account is on the server the site is served from, whose name stands over the form. A site without a server says so instead of offering a form.
- **Refusals stand where they belong**, not in an alert: under the fields of the form, in the dialog that asks for the password, among the rows about the logbook. The words are the core's.
- **What the last sync did** is a row of its own: "3 sent, 1 fetched".
- **The link** of a shared flight opens its page in a new tab, and a button copies it; a browser has no share sheet to hand it to.
- **Deleting a flight** says, when signed in, that the server's copy and those on the pilot's other devices go with it.
- **Who may register is the server's to say**, and the form for a new account follows it. Where the server takes accounts by invite, the form has the field for the code and says who hands codes out. Where anyone may register, there is no such field and no word about invites. Where the server takes no new accounts, "New account" says so and has no form: "This server takes no new accounts. An account you already have signs in as before." A server that does not say, as an older one, may ask for a code, so the field is offered as it always was. The iPhone and Android show the field in every case.
- **Passwords** are fields a password manager recognises: the handle as the user name, the password as a current or a new one.

Keyboard and screen reader: everything can be reached and used from the keyboard. Times are shown in the time zone of the browser, as the iPhone shows them in the phone's. The words are English, and so are the numbers on every platform: a point before the decimals and a comma between thousands, whatever region the device is set to. Heights are the exception, as the design pack writes them: no mark for thousands ("2410 m", "+1140 m") and a loss with the true minus sign ("−135 m"); on the web since 2026-11-20, as on the phones. Climb and sink rates take the same minus, always with a sign: "+2.4 m/s", "−1.9 m/s". Counts and distances keep their mark. The core words its own numbers that way ("10.0 km NE"), and a screen that wrote its own by the region showed "2,5 km" beside them on a device set to Germany. A time of day is written as the region writes one, which is what the iPhone asks its system for: on a 24-hour clock the hour has two digits, "00:05" and "09:30", and never "0:05", which is how an airtime is written.

## How a design change is checked

Nobody on the project can open Xcode in a cloud session, so the interface is checked by tests that use it. `apps/ios/VentiUITests` starts the app with an empty logbook, taps through every screen as a pilot would, asserts what should be there, and keeps a screenshot of each screen in light and dark appearance. The iOS workflow publishes them as the `ios-screenshots` artifact. Look at them after every change to a screen.

The Android app is checked the same way by `apps/android/app/src/androidTest`, on an emulator that only GitHub can run; the Android workflow publishes the screenshots as the `android-screenshots` artifact.

The web app is checked the same way by `apps/web/e2e`, in a real browser, at a phone's width and a desktop's, in light and dark. A cloud session can run these itself (`npm run test:e2e` in `apps/web`) and look at `apps/web/screenshots`; the Web workflow publishes them as the `web-screenshots` artifact.

## The cockpit

The first version is built and has been checked on a simulator with a replayed flight. It has not flown.

Since 2026-11-20 the iPhone's cockpit is laid out as the design language's flight screen (`design/reference/screens/fly.png`), in its flight theme, and this section describes that. Android has the same layout since the same day; its section says what differs.

- **A full-screen cover, not a tab.** Opening the cockpit covers the whole app; nothing can be navigated to by accident. A small "End" at the right of the top line ends it, held for 0.7 s while it fills (two-thirds of the second it was, the owner, 2026-11-23), and nothing asks again (since 2026-11-21: the owner, of the two large buttons that stood at the foot of every page, "a huge waste ... Phone real estate is extremely important there"). A pilot who has the screen read out ends with the usual double tap.
- **The flight theme, always.** A white ground and near-black ink whatever the phone is set to, no shadows, no text under 14 px, and every sheet the cockpit opens in the same theme. What floats over the map has an edge in place of a shadow.
- **It records by itself.** The Fly tab's button is "Open the cockpit", not "Start flight". The cockpit waits on the ground ("Ready for take-off. Recording starts by itself.") and writes nothing; when the flight computer notices the take-off the flight file begins, with the half minute before it, and live tracking with it. Two minutes after the glider has come to rest the flight is saved to the logbook and shown, as if the pilot had ended it; the notice at the top counts down ("Landed. The flight is saved in 1:32."). A cockpit that has waited two hours without a take-off closes and says so: waiting keeps the receivers on. "Record now" on the flight's page is for a pilot who would rather not wait for the take-off to be noticed. The rules are the core's (`core::cockpit`): 30 s of lead, 120 s after landing, two hours of waiting, and a hop of less than 30 s from take-off to coming to rest is thrown away and the cockpit waits again, so that ground handling leaves nothing in the logbook. A take-off is 4 m/s over the ground for 5 s, or 25 m of height gained or lost steadily over half a minute; a receiver that settles by tens of metres in a step, or finds out that it is somewhere else, has not taken off, and the cockpit takes only fixes of now that are good to 100 m. To verify in the air; a glider parked in a strong wind for two minutes would be taken for landed. Since 2026-11-22 Android's Fly tab keeps one paragraph of small print under its lines; the iPhone's: to verify.
- **Pages by a swipe.** A sideways swipe goes to the next or the previous page (around them), and the page dots in the top line go to the next page when tapped, so nothing needs a swipe; nothing stands at the foot of a page, whose height goes to the readings and the map. With a task flown, a "Task" page gives the next turnpoint's and goal's readings. The first page is the readings over the map. The second is the map as large as it gets, with the line that says where to fly, the task's line and three small readings under it, and the round buttons for the whole flight and for the view from the side over it. The third is the flight: its time, the cross-country distance so far and how it scores ("Triangle, 1.2 km to close"), and what can be switched: the vario's sound, the lock, live tracking.
- **The Fly tab is the cockpit** (owner decision 23; iPhone and Android, 2026-11-22). Choosing the tab opens the cockpit; there is no screen before it. What that screen held (the vario and its device, the thermal map, airspace, task, live tracking, the offline map, the glider, locking the screen, replaying the sample flight) is behind one gear on the cockpit's top line, in a sheet in the flight theme. A check that fails speaks on the top line as sign and word, and goes when it is fixed: "No GPS", "No airspace", "No offline map" (on the ground only); nothing is said when all is well. The vario's state is a small mark there with the pack's vario sign: the sign alone when connected, with "Connecting", "Vario lost" or "No Bluetooth" otherwise, nothing when the pilot flies with the phone alone. While the app is in front, on any tab, it watches for a take-off with the core's own detection, once location is allowed; a take-off opens the cockpit and records. A take-off the core then takes back (a cable car, a drive) drops its recording and leaves the cockpit quietly. The thermal map of thermal.kk7.ch is drawn on the cockpit's map at 40 %, on by default, with its switch in the sheet, fetched only with a network.
- **Nothing but flying on the cockpit's map** (the owner, 2026-11-23, of credit boxes over the map: "real estate is precious. You cannot put this huge box with completely useless information"; "and also airspace, we don't need it there"). No credit or notice is drawn over the cockpit's map on any page: not the airspace's ("openAIP, not certified"), not the thermal map's, the cables', the sites', other aircraft's or climbs', not the aerial photographs'. All of them are behind the small (i) at the map's lower left, which lists every one that applies at the moment.
- **The thermal view beside the map** (the owner, 2026-11-20: "the thermal assistant should be beside the map", as on the first page of the layout they fly with, [xctrack-parity.md](xctrack-parity.md#a-layout-a-pilot-flies-with-the-owners)). On the first page the room under the readings is shared: on the left, a little under half the width, a close-up of the air the glider is in; on the right the map. The close-up is drawn, not a map: north up, the flight theme's ground and ink, an edge and no shadow, no tiles, so it is there without a network and reads in the sun. It is always there, on the ground, on a glide and circling (for an afternoon of 2026-11-22 a session had it show only while circling, against the owner's word; the owner: "I don't see the thermal assistant in the map. It should also be there, and very important, the thermal assistant also shows the wind direction and intensity"). A corner of it gives the wind as a large arrow and its speed in km/h; before the first circle has measured one, the forecast's wind at the level nearest the glider's height for this hour, marked "forecast".
  - *While the glider circles* it shows the core's picture of the lift (`lift`): the places flown through in the last fifty seconds, each where its air is now, as dots in the climb colours, larger where this thermal climbs better; the circle to fly next as a dashed line with a mark at its middle; the glider as the own-position arrow, pointing where it flies. It is as wide as the circles need and never closer than 150 m across.
  - *On the glide* it shows the last three minutes of track (`trail`) in the climb colours, 600 m across with the glider in the middle, and the thermals left behind with their climb: where the lift was, for going back to it.
  - In its corners: the wind as an arrow that points where the air moves, with its speed, and a scale ("100 m"). Under it the words about the centre ("Stronger 20 m NW", "Centred") while there are any.
  - The map beside it stays at its width for the glide and draws the track and the thermals, not the dots: the close-up is where the circle is looked at. The second page's large map zooms in while circling as before.
  - The rules of what is shown are the core's (`lift`, `trail`, `thermals`, `wind`); the view only places them. Nothing in it is tapped.
  - Built on the iPhone on 2026-11-20 (`ThermalCloseUp`), seen in a replayed flight on a simulator; Android follows. Not flown.
- **Whose data the map draws.** Airspace fetched from the pilot's server is credited in a small line in the map's lower left corner, beside the base map's own credits, with "not certified" where its provider asks for that: the licence wants the credit next to the data, and in the air the map is where the data is.
- **Fixed places, fixed sizes.** The climb is the one very large reading (`instrument-xl`, 84 px), with its name and the average of the last twenty seconds under it. Under it stand four large ones (`instrument-l`, 44 px) in two rows: altitude with the height over the ground, ground speed, glide, and the wind with where it comes from ("Wind from W"). The number comes first and its name under it. Unlike everywhere else, these sizes do not follow the text-size setting: an instrument must be where the eye expects it. A value the sensors have not supplied is two dashes, never an old number: five seconds without a position and the speed, the glide and, without a pressure sensor, the altitude and the climb are dashes, and the line at the top says "No GPS". A wind from a while ago has its age under it ("12 min old"). Heights have no mark between the thousands ("2410 m"), as the design language writes them, and a loss has the true minus sign.
- **It changes by itself.** While the glider circles in lift, ground speed and glide give way to the height gained and the thermal's average climb, and the map zooms in; on the glide they come back, with the glide ratio the glider needs to reach the place it is flying to under the one it is making ("Needs 6.5"), and the map zooms out. Altitude and wind never move. The pilot touches nothing.
- **Where to.** One line under the map of the second page says where the cockpit is navigating: an arrow that points to the place as seen from the glider (up is straight ahead), its name and distance, and the height at which the glider is expected to arrive over it. Until the pilot chooses a place, that place is the take-off. A tap on the line lists the pilot's waypoints nearest first; that list is the one thing in the cockpit that needs a second touch. On the map a thin dashed line runs from the glider to the place. An arrival below the place is the second thing shown in red, and a wind too strong to get there is said in words, not as a number. When no wind has been measured yet, the line says that the prediction is for still air.
- **The map follows.** North up, the glider in the middle, the last three minutes of track behind it in the climb colours. It cannot be dragged away; it can be zoomed.
- **The climb's scale** beside the climb number is the design language's: three steps above a line for the climb and three below for the sink, each lit in its colour of the vario scale once the glider climbs or sinks that fast (0.5, 1.5 and 3 m/s). It is read by its shape, more steps for more climb; the number itself stays in the text colour.
- **The line at the top** says whether the cockpit is ready, recording or replaying, how long the flight has lasted, and on the right where the climb comes from. The cross-country distance is on the flight's page.
- **Daylight.** Once less than two hours are left, the top left of the map says "Sunset in 1:12"; after sunset "Night in 0:20", in red, until the end of civil twilight. The times are for a level horizon: in a valley the sun is gone earlier.
- **What is missing is said in red at the top:** "No GPS", or a vario that has stopped talking.
- **Airspace.** The most urgent warning stands at the top of the screen, on every page, as a band with the design language's airspace sign: what and where in one line ("CTR Innsbruck 300 m NE", "CTR Innsbruck in 30 s", "Inside CTR Innsbruck"), its class and limits below, and a line when the height is in doubt. Plain while the airspace is near, on the caution colour when very near, on the alert red (`danger-fill`) when the glider is about to be inside or is; the words say which. A warning of a strong wind at the landing stands under it on the caution colour, and under that the notice of a cockpit that is ready or has landed. When a warning takes room at the top the map gives way; the readings keep their size and their place. A new or more urgent warning is spoken, because the pilot's eyes are outside. A tap lists all warnings with two answers: "Seen: quiet for 10 minutes", which speaks again if it gets worse, and "Does not apply today". On the maps airspace to stay out of has a magenta edge and a faint wash, as aviation charts draw boundaries; airspace that deserves attention is amber; the rest a thin grey line without a name. The wash fades in between zoom 8 and 10: on a map of a whole region, where airspace lies over airspace, only the edges are drawn, so that the wind and the valleys are not painted over. A round button over the map of the second page shows the whole flight in one view, with the scored route and, once the flight has covered three kilometres in a straight line, the two FAI triangle areas: dashed outlines on either side of that line, inside which a turnpoint makes it one side of an FAI triangle. A second tap goes back to the close view that follows the glider. Since 2026-11-22 the two areas are drawn only where the pilot has switched "FAI triangle areas" on in the settings, off by default, on both phones. The areas can be switched off in the settings. At the map's upper left stands the landing to think of first, once the pilot keeps landings among the waypoints: the one the glider is expected to arrive highest above, with that height ("Sample landing field +510 m"). It is plain while there are a hundred metres or more to spare, amber with less, and it turns red and says "No landing in reach" when the glide reaches none. The same height stands beside every place in the list to fly to, which a tap on the badge opens. The prediction is the one the cockpit makes for the place it navigates to: the glider's trim speed and glide ratio from the settings, the wind last measured, a straight line. The ground in between is not looked at, and the list says so. The wind is the one last measured: on the first page it is a reading, on the others a badge over the map with the design language's wind arrow, which points where the air goes; once the measurement is five minutes old its age stands beside it ("11 km/h 12 min"), because by then it is a memory of another place and height. Under it an amber line appears while the wind last measured, if it is not older than twenty minutes, leaves the glider less than 10 km/h of way into it at the trim speed set for the glider: "Into the wind 4 km/h", or "Wind as strong as the glider is fast". It is a statement about the trim speed the pilot entered; with the speed bar a glider makes more. A tap on the wind, reading or badge, lists the wind by height: one row for each band of 200 m the glider has circled in, the highest first, with the direction the air moves, the speed, how long ago it was measured, which band the glider is in now, and under the wind how fast the glider climbed while it circled through the band, in the colour of that climb: where the thermal was strong and where it weakened. The wind in the valley and the wind above the ridges are two winds. The thermals the glider has left stay on the cockpit's map, the last twelve that gave fifty metres or more: a dot in the colour of climb with the average climb beside it ("+2.4"), where the glider circled. While the glider circles, the map pictures the thermal it is in. The last fifty seconds are drawn as dots, one a second, each moved with the wind to where the air it was flown through is now: over the ground a climb in wind is a line of loops, in the air it is the circles the pilot flew, and that is where the lift stays. A dot has the colour of its climb, as the trail has, and is larger where the climb is better than the thermal's mean and smaller where it is worse; an even thermal is a string of equal beads. Around where the lift is lies a dashed ring with a dot in its middle: the circle to fly, as large as the circles flown and moved toward the stronger side, by no more than its own radius. It lies under the dots, so a circle that is centred covers it. Beside the wind over the map a second badge says the same in words, with an arrow that points the way on the north-up map: "Stronger 20 m NW", or "Centred" with a target sign when the lift lies within a fifth of the radius. The picture appears once three quarters of a circle are flown and goes when the thermal is left. Until the first circle has given a wind the dots are where the glider was over the ground. The trail over the ground stays drawn beneath. Before take-off the Fly tab says in a line what the cockpit will warn from ("openAIP, 344 airspaces · 1 file", or "None"), and a tap opens the list: what is kept from the pilot's server, by provider, with its date, its credit and its notice, and the pilot's own files. The cockpit must never be silent for a reason the pilot could not have seen on the ground.
- **The airspace that matters next, always there.** *Built on the iPhone and on Android on 2026-11-21 as proposed here, for the owner to see and to move; seen in each phone's UI tests with a made-up zone, not flown.* The band above speaks when something is urgent. The owner's XCTrack layout also has a reading that stands all the time: the airspace proximity, small on its first page and large on the page with the cross-country map ([xctrack-parity.md](xctrack-parity.md#a-layout-a-pilot-flies-with-the-owners)). The cockpit is to have both. What it says is the core's, in the view as `airspace_proximity`: the one airspace that matters next out to 5 km over the ground, how far its outline is and in which direction, and how it lies in height ("CTR Innsbruck · 3.2 km NE · floor 400 m above"; "TMA Innsbruck · overhead · floor 324 m above"; "CTR Innsbruck · inside, edge 2.2 km N"). Which airspace that is among several, and what the reading does not know, is in [airspace.md](airspace.md#the-airspace-that-matters-next). A shell places the words and reckons nothing: no distance, no direction and no choice of airspace is worked out on the phone.
  - *Small, over a corner of the first page's map.* The airspace sign and two short lines: where (`place_words`: "3.2 km NE") and how high (`height_words`: "floor 400 m above"), or the two in one line where the map is wide enough (`short_words`). The name is left to the map and to the large reading. It has an edge and no shadow, like everything that floats over the map. It stands in the lower right corner on both phones: the upper left has the landing and the daylight, the lower left the credits. Nothing in it is tapped. On a phone the first page's map is half the width, so the two-line form is what shows.
  - *Large, on the map's page.* The name (`name`), where and how high as two readings beside each other (`place_words`, `height_words`), and under them the height line: the airspace's class and limits as published (`detail`: "Class D control zone · GND to 5000 ft AMSL"). For a drawn scale in its place the view has the glider's altitude and the airspace's floor and ceiling on one scale (`altitude_m`, `floor_m`, `ceiling_m`). What the "height line" of XCTrack's widget shows was not looked up: *to verify* on the owner's phone. A tap opens the list of warnings, as a tap on the band does (on the iPhone; Android has no tap yet). Both phones draw the scale, and both put the reading in a strip of its own directly under the map, above the line to the target, so that nothing over the map is covered or moved: the map gives up that height, as it does to the band. With the glider inside a zone the band above the map and the strip under it are both on the alert red, which leaves the map short; whether the strip then gives way is the owner's to say.
  - *It stays while a warning is up,* and is then as urgent as the band: plain while the airspace is near, on the caution colour when very near, on the alert red when the glider is about to be inside or is (`level`). With no warning for its airspace it is plain; an airspace that only deserves attention has the amber of the map (`policy`). One the pilot has quieted is drawn as the quieted band is (`silenced`). The band and the reading can be about two airspaces: the band about the most urgent warning, the reading about where the glider is now.
  - *Doubt is in the words.* When the height cannot be placed the words say "possibly at your height" or "possibly inside"; the large reading has room for the line that says what is missing (`uncertain`).
  - *No reading is not "clear".* With none (`airspace_proximity` absent) neither strip is drawn, on both phones (since 2026-11-23 on the iPhone, where the large one showed two dashes before), so that a pilot without a reading loses no map to it; the top line's "No airspace" says when the cockpit has none. Neither says that the air is free: none within 5 km is what an empty reading means only while the cockpit is checking, and whether it is checking is said where it is said today.
- **The score beside the map.** On the map's page a small badge says what the flight scores so far and what it scores as ("6.1 km · Free flight"), and for a triangle that is not closed how far there is to fly to close it: the third page's words, now also where the pilot looks at the map. On both phones since 2026-11-21; at the upper left under the landing on the iPhone, at the lower right on Android, which is one of the two to settle.
- **The course, written.** *Built on both phones on 2026-11-21.* The owner's layout has the bearing as a small reading under the ground speed. Ours: the small line under the ground speed, where the altitude has its height over the ground, in the core's words (`course_words`: "285° W", whole degrees and one of eight compass points). The core leaves it out while the glider moves slower than 2 m/s over the ground, where a course says nothing, and the line is then empty. While the glider circles the ground speed gives way to the thermal's gain, and the course with it.
- **The competition page's readings.** *In the flight engine and the core since 2026-11-21; on no screen yet. What follows for the screens is a proposal, for the owner to see and to move.* The owner's XCTrack layout has a page for a competition task ([xctrack-parity.md](xctrack-parity.md#a-layout-a-pilot-flies-with-the-owners), page 2). The core gives its readings with every view, as `CockpitView::task_readings`, absent without a task:
  - *For the next turnpoint and for goal* (`next`, `goal`, each a `TaskLegReading`): the distance along the shortest way through the cylinders; the height to arrive at (`arrive_at_m`: the turnpoint's height as the task gives it, for goal with the finish altitude on top, none for the start and for a route's waypoint written with zero); the altitude needed now to arrive there (`altitude_needed_m`: that height plus what the glide along the shortest way costs, leg by leg, at the glider's trim speed in the wind last worked out, `venti_flight::task::glide`); how far over or under that the glider is (`over_needed_m`, in words `over_needed_words`: "+180 m"); the glide needed to the height to arrive at (`glide_needed`); and the time of arrival (`arrives_unix_s`, `arrives_in_s`) at the speed section's average once the clock has run a minute, else at the speed over the ground while not circling. All of it in one line, `words`: "WEST · 12.4 km · +180 m · glide 8.2 · in 23 min". Whether a turnpoint's own height is the right one to arrive at, the edge of its cylinder lying elsewhere, is *to verify* against XCTrack.
  - *Before the start* (`start`): the time to the gate (`opens_in_s`), the time to the start's edge at the speed over the ground (`arrives_in_s`) and by how much that is early (`early_by_s`), in words: "Start opens in 12:30; you arrive 3 min early", "…; you arrive 2 min after it opens", "Start is open; next gate in 15:00". Within half a minute of the gate it is "you arrive as it opens".
  - *The speed section's average* (`speed_section_average_ms`, "31.2 km/h"): the part of its shortest way flown over the time on the clock, from a minute after the start; once the section is flown, its length over its time.
  - *Proposal for the screens:* a fourth page, "Task", as XCTrack's page 2: a row of altitude, the next turnpoint's `over_needed_words` and its time of arrival; the task map as on the map's page; the start's words laid on the map's corner until the start is taken; under the map goal's distance, glide needed and `over_needed_words`, and the average speed. Without a task the page is not there. "Still air" beside the altitudes needed while `wind_known` is false.
- **The closing circle.** *In the flight engine and the core since 2026-11-21; on no screen yet; the screens are a proposal.* For a triangle still open, `FlightSession::live_score` carries `closing` (`ClosingCircleView`): the circle around the triangle's start that its finish has to come into, by the rule the engine scores with, XContest's (a twentieth of the perimeter; a fifth is where it counts as a triangle at all, `counts_radius_m`), as a centre, a radius and an outline to draw; the distance from the glider to its edge; and what the triangle scores closed on that edge, the least a closed one of these turnpoints scores ("Closing circle 2.1 km away: closed FAI triangle of 57.0 km, 91.2 points"). It is there also while a free flight scores more, as long as the flight makes a triangle that counts, and gone once the triangle is closed. The route planner's `RoutePlan` has the same circle (`closing_circle`) when the drawn route makes a triangle, without a distance ("Closes within 1.6 km of the start: …"). *Proposal:* on the map's page, the circle as a thin dashed ring in the map's ink with the FAI areas, and its words under the score's badge.
- **Take-offs and others on the cockpit's map.** *In the core since 2026-11-21; on no screen yet; the screens are a proposal.* `Core::cockpit_takeoffs` gives the take-offs the app holds around the glider, nearest first and fifty at most, each with its distance and direction and its credits: the pilot's own, which need no network, and those of the server's answer for `today_area` around the glider, which the shell asks for again once the glider has left the area it asked for. Discouraged ones and those from pilots are among them, each record saying what it is. For other pilots, the shell asks `cockpit_others_path` (30 km around the glider) every ten to fifteen seconds in the air and hands each answer to `FlightSession::on_others`; `FlightSession::others_near` gives the aircraft within 30 km, nearest first, fifty at most, each with how far above or below the glider it is and its track of the last five minutes, which the session keeps from the answers (the server keeps no tracks), with the network's credit. The pilot's own FLARM heard by the network is not told apart. *Proposal:* take-offs as the map tab's small take-off sign, without names until zoomed in; others as the live map's marks with a thin track in the map's ink and the height difference beside them ("+300").
- **The smart readings of 2026-11-22.** *In the flight engine and the core since 2026-11-22 (`f9e8968`); on Android (`38714f0`) and the iPhone (`9382b64`) the same night, as each bullet says, for the owner to see and to move. Most of it has been seen on no screen: the sample flight's replay has no landing site near it, no pressure and no glider class, so the nearest landing's line, the band of the wind carrying back, the QNH and the speed to fly are unit-tested only.* By the owner's word of that day ("advanced features should either be seamless or be available from a specific view or toggle able with a setting") each is one of three: **seamless** (it makes better what the cockpit shows already, with no new control), **a view of its own** (a page a pilot adds), or **behind a setting** that is off until the pilot turns it on. None puts a new reading on the first page.
  - *Wind on a glide: seamless.* The wind is no longer only the last circles': a turn of a third of a circle or more within a minute of glide gives one too, and the cockpit goes by whichever it trusts more now (a measurement loses half its trust every five minutes; a glide's is never trusted more than the share of a circle it saw). `CockpitView::wind` is that wind, `wind_source` says which, and `wind_words` says it in a line: "11 km/h from W, from your last glide, 2 min old". A straight line gives no wind. As built on both phones: the wind reading stays where it is, and `wind_words` is the first row of the sheet "Wind by height" that the wind reading opens. The proposal's short form under the reading itself is not built.
  - *Airspeed and heading, estimated: a view of its own.* `airspeed_ms` and `heading_deg`, with `airspeed_words` ("about 36 km/h") and `heading_words` ("about 350° N"): the way over the ground less the wind, absent on the ground, without a wind and with one older than twenty minutes. As built on both phones: two rows of a "Details" sheet, opened from a row on the cockpit's third page (`cockpit-details`), with the altitude's source, the QNH and netto; on no page of readings. Laying out a page of one's own is not built.
  - *Carried backwards: seamless.* `carried_back`, "The wind carries you back: 4 km/h backwards", while on a glide the glider moves over the ground against the direction it points in. As built on both phones: a band among the cockpit's notices at the top (`carried-back`), in the caution colour, while it is said. Not seen on a screen.
  - *The nearest landing within glide: seamless once the app hands over landings.* `FlightSession::use_landings` takes the landings among the sites the app holds around the glider (`Core::cockpit_takeoffs`' sites and the pilot's own); `CockpitView::nearest_landing` is then the nearest one the straight glide reaches with 100 m to spare in this wind and with no ground in the way: "Landing Musterwiese: 2.1 km, +340 m". When none is: "No landing within glide: Musterwiese 6.2 km, 180 m short", or "…, a ridge in the way". As built on both phones: one line on the map page (`nearest-landing`), on the iPhone under the landing badge and on Android under the second page's map, on the caution's ground where none is within glide; the landings handed over are the core's pick (`Core::cockpit_landings`). The proposal's band at the top for the caution is not built: the caution stays in that line. No new control. Not seen on a screen.
  - *Landing detection: seamless, and one setting.* A glider standing still over the ground is no longer landed after twenty seconds when the ground is known to lie more than 50 m below it (then only after five minutes), or when a wind of 18 km/h or more was measured in the last twenty minutes (then after a minute). `FlightSession::set_never_land` is the setting, off by default: "Never land (hike and fly)" under the glider in the settings on both phones, on the settings' page "Glider and cockpit". `CockpitView::never_lands` says it is on; the small mark in the top line is not built on either phone.
  - *The cable warning follows the glide: seamless.* The same band and the same words; it now comes for a glider that is high above a cable and sinking toward it, and looks wider on the side the glider turns to. `CableWarning::arrival_above_m` is the height it arrives with.
  - *A polar, netto and the speed to fly: behind a setting.* Nothing of it shows until the pilot sets a glider class (`FlightSession::set_glider_class`: EN A, B, C, D or CCC) or their own polar (`set_polar`: trim and top speed, each with its sink). Then `netto_ms` and `netto_words` ("air −1.3 m/s") say what the air itself does on a glide, `speed_to_fly` gives a hint in words ("Speed up: sinking air and head wind", "Trim: you are in rising air", "Trim: tail wind") with `bar`, the share of the speed bar, and arrival heights, the glide range and a task's altitude needed are worked out at the speed that carries furthest in the wind in place of trim speed. As built on both phones: "Glider class" is a setting under the glider, none until chosen (`glider-class`); with it set, the hint is the climb reading's note while it says to speed up, and netto a row of the "Details" sheet. Not seen on a screen. `set_polar` has no setting on either phone. The classes' numbers are the project's own, to verify with pilots.
  - *QNH: a view of its own.* `qnh_hpa`, `qnh_altitude_m` and `qnh_words` ("QNH 1018 hPa, from the ground at take-off · airspace above sea level measured at it", "…, set by hand" after `FlightSession::set_qnh`, "… · no pressure now: airspace above sea level by GPS" without a fresh pressure), and `altitude_source_words` ("Altitude from the pressure sensor, levelled to GPS", "Altitude from GPS"). As built on both phones: rows of the "Details" sheet (`qnh_words`, `altitude_source_words`), and the QNH words say what the airspace warnings go by (`d81b8a7`). Setting the QNH by hand is on no screen of either phone. The airspace warnings measure limits above sea level against `qnh_altitude_m` when it is there and everything above ground against `altitude_m`: see [airspace.md](airspace.md) and the decision log.
  - *Cross-country and task arithmetic: engine only.* Leg lengths with their shares and the average cross-country speed (`venti_flight::scoring::legs`, `average_speed_ms`), and for a task the height kept back per turnpoint and the choice of the speed an arrival time is taken from (`venti_flight::task::glide`), are in the flight engine and on no reading of the core yet. They are for the "Task" page proposed above and for a cross-country page a pilot adds, never the first page.
- **What went wrong** while the cockpit is open (the position refused, a link for the flight that could not be made) is a band among these notices, with the caution sign, in words, and a tap puts it away. It is not a box over the screen: that would hide the readings, and one raised while the cockpit opens kept it from opening. Outside the cockpit the same is said in the system's alert.
- **Sound.** Beeps that rise in pitch and speed with the climb, a continuous low tone in heavy sink, silence on the ground. The core decides when the phone sounds (`CockpitView.phone_tone`): only in flight, and not while the external vario the pilot chose in the cockpit's settings is connected, since it beeps itself; when its link drops in the air the phone's pressure sensor and tone take over at once, "Vario lost" is said once, and the phone falls silent again when the vario is back (owner decision 23). There is no mute button. The climb never comes from the satellite height alone: without a pressure sensor or a vario the cockpit shows "– –" and plays nothing.
- **Trying it on the ground.** The Fly tab can replay the sample flight through the cockpit, in real time or twenty times faster.

To verify in the air, all of it: readability in sun, whether the numbers are the right ones, the sound, glove-sized targets.

What the apps pilots already fly with teach, and what the next versions should take from them:

- XCTrack switches to its thermal page by itself when it detects circling and back afterwards, and offers black themes and a white high-contrast one. Automatic actions exist "to minimize in-flight user interaction".
- SeeYou Navigator lays values over the map and lets any box be moved and resized by pressing and holding. Its reviewer chose the light map "which lets you see the screen better", and missed an automatic re-centre.
- burnair shortens the pilot's own track to the last minute and a half while circling, to clear the map.
- ForeFlight fixes its hazard colours (red within 100 ft, yellow within 1,000 ft) and repeats every alert in sound.

**Locked.** A button with a lock, on the flight's page, makes the screen deaf to touches: a glove, a line or rain on the glass cannot press a button, and the button that ends the flight least of all. The cockpit goes on showing and sounding as before. A lock in the place of the two buttons says so and opens it when a finger is held on it for a second. Not tried with gloves or in rain.

**In the sun.** While the cockpit is open the screen is turned all the way up, and back to where it was afterwards; a switch in the settings leaves the brightness alone for a pilot who would rather keep the battery. The cockpit is always in the flight theme, which is drawn for the sun; the switch "Cockpit for bright sun" that came before it is gone. The map is still the ordinary map. Not tried in the sun.

Still to design: a map drawn for the sun, layouts the pilot can change, routes of more than one place. The sheets the cockpit opens are lists in small type and not yet flight screens: the wind by height, the airspace warnings, the places to fly to.

### A task in the cockpit

The Fly tab has a line for the task beside those for the vario and the airspace: which task the cockpit will fly, or "None". It opens the task's page: a sketch of the task to scale without a map, north up, with the shortest way through it in the accent colour, the start cylinder dashed because it is the one with a clock on it and the goal filled; under it every turnpoint with what it is, its radius, how far along the task it lies and the leg into it; then the day's times on the iPhone's clock. A task comes in from the clipboard, from a file, or by scanning its QR code with the camera, and goes out, from the page's menu, as a QR code or a file. A task file opened from Files or Mail with "Open in Venti" is kept at once, and the app says so on the Fly tab. A route is made on the same page from the pilot's own waypoints: they are tapped in the order to fly them, the route so far stands above the list with a number for each stop, and a waypoint may be in it twice, since out and back is a route too. A route can also be drawn without waypoints ("Draw a route on the map"): the map takes the whole screen, every tap is the next place, numbered on the map with a line through them, and a panel at the bottom says what the route comes to ("30.1 km along the route. Flown like this it scores as a closed FAI triangle: 30.1 km, 48.2 points.") with the legs in a line under it ("1  10.0 km E · 2  10.0 km NW"). What it would score is worked out as for a flight that was flown, with the best start and finish found on the drawn line; that route is drawn dashed in the accent colour over the line, and nobody has to say that a route is a triangle. "Back to the start" adds the first place again, since a finger does not hit it twice; "Undo" takes the last place away; a tap closer than 150 m to the place before counts as the same tap. Kept, the places are named "Start", "Turnpoint 1" and so on to "Finish", and the cockpit leads along them. With one leg drawn, the map shows the two areas in which a third place makes an FAI triangle of it, dashed as in the cockpit, and the panel says how large such a triangle is ("of 23 to 36 km"). A place cannot be moved once tapped. The code is drawn dark on white in both appearances, because that is what scanners read.

- **A route with the weather along it** (in the server and the core since 2026-11-21; in the planners of the three apps since the same day, the web's planner new with it). For a drawn route, a start time, the pilot's mean speed over the ground (climbs included) and a glide ratio, the core says when the pilot would be at each place, and from the server's forecast along the route (`GET /v1/route/weather`, the thermal map's hours at the route's squares, [api.md](api.md#get-v1routeweatherdatepoints)) what is there then: at each place a line ("Wallberg at 10:00: weak thermals. 9 km/h from the SSW at 1570 m. Overcast."), the height to leave it at to reach the next with 200 m over the ground on the line, in the leg's wind ("Leave Wallberg at 1920 m to reach Rottach with 200 m to spare in still air."; the slope under the summit decides it), a line per leg with its wind as a head, tail or cross wind ("The first leg, 9.8 km to Spitzing from 10:00 to 10:29: 8 km/h cross wind from the right."; a leg that meets rain adds "It runs into rain by 13:00.") and a line for the whole route (how long, from when to when, the first leg into rain, the places whose hour is ruled out, the strongest head wind, an end after the last daylight hour). The wind is read at the height the pilot set, else at the thermals' top, else 500 m over the model's ground. The rules are in the head of `crates/core/src/route_weather.rs`; the calls are `route_weather_path`, `RouteForecast::read` and `route_along` (on the web `routeWeatherPath`, `new RouteForecast`, `RouteForecast.along` and `routeAlong`), and the web has `planRoute` now too. Without a forecast the times and still-air heights stand; without the ground loaded the heights say so. *A proposal for the screens, not decided:* the planner's panel gets a row of three settings over its legs, the start (a time on the pilot's clock, today or a day the map has), the speed (km/h, the pilot's, kept like the glide) and the glide (the cockpit's own setting); each leg's line is the core's, with the head wind as a small arrow in ink beside it, and the route's line heads the panel; the numbered places on the map carry their departure heights as labels ("1920 m"), and a place whose hour is ruled out takes the not-flyable status; while the route is a triangle of one leg the FAI areas stay drawn as they are, and with three places the closing circle (5% of the perimeter around the start) is drawn dashed. The web gets the planner it does not have yet, from the map tab's "Plan route", the same panel beside the map on a wide screen and as a sheet on a phone.
- **A route as a GPX file, out and in** (in the core since 2026-11-22, `c67cd9c`; in the planners of the three apps the same night: Android `641bfde`, the web `d34c0da`, the iPhone `e62862c`). `route_to_gpx` writes the planner's places as one GPX 1.1 route whose points are named "Start", "Turnpoint 1" … "Goal", with Venti as its creator and nothing of the pilot's (no time, no name, no device); `route_from_gpx` reads a file's first route, else its waypoints in their order, else its track thinned to the planner's 25 places (the first and the last, then one at a time the place farthest off the line through those kept), and says what it read in a line ("A track of 3,402 points, thinned to 25 places.") or refuses in words ("This file holds no route."). The rules and the hand-written reader's limits are in the head of `crates/core/src/route_file.rs`; on the web `routeToGpx` and `routeFromGpx`. *As built:* "Export GPX" and "Import GPX" stand in the planner's menu on each app (the iPhone's "More", Android's menu, the menu in the head of the web's route card), nothing on the planner's default screen. "Export GPX" hands the file to the system's sheet (the iPhone's sheet that saves a file, Android's share sheet, on the web a download); "Import GPX" opens the system's file picker and puts the places read in place of the route, and the core's line stands over the route's words until the route changes (on the web once, under the title). Where a route is drawn already, the core's question comes first on all three (`planner_words()`: "Replace the route you have drawn with the file’s?", "Replace" or "Cancel"), and the route stays as it was on "Cancel": on the phones where more than the take-off the route began at is drawn (iPhone `9de3aaa9`, Android `9013e2a4`), on the web where any place is (since the last round of 2026-11-22). On the phones no UI test answers the question, since none picks a file; the web's browser test answers it both ways (`route-planner.spec.ts`, screenshot `218-route-gpx-replace-question`; seen passing: to verify). A file that cannot be read is refused in the core's words and the route stays (seen on the web; on the phones no UI test picks a file).

- **What a glide reaches** (in the flight engine and the core since 2026-11-21; on the three apps since the same day: a long press on a phone, a click on the web, opens "What a glide reaches from here"). From any place on the map, for the pilot's glide ratio (the cockpit's own setting), a start height or none, a wind or none, the core lays a straight glide over the ground the shell loaded every 5° and gives the area it reaches as a closed ring, each direction's distance, arrival height and height over the ground, why it ends there (the ground, the edge of the ground loaded, or a wind it makes no way against), the farthest and nearest reach, and the answer in words: "From 1717 m at a glide of 8 you reach 6.3 km to the north-west and 230 m to the south-west, 200 m over the ground. To the north-east, east, south-west and west the ground falls away too slowly to glide off." (the Wallberg's summit over the region that comes with the iPhone app), with "Ground not loaded beyond the edge of the map to the east." where a line runs off the tiles, and a note of what it does not know: lift and sink, a wind that changes with height, a lee, a ridge narrower than the 100 m the ground is read in; nothing flies round a ridge. Without a start height the glide starts on the ground there, as from a summit walked up to; it keeps 200 m over the ground, the planned route's margin, and a glide begun on the ground has that only from 1 km out, the margin growing to it on the way, so a slope must fall away by some 18° for a glide of 8 to leave in its direction. The same engine draws the cockpit's own reach in flight, with its 100 m. For a map of slopes the core gives each place's steepness over 50 m and its class: under 25°, 25–30°, 30–35°, 35–40°, 40° and more, the classes avalanche maps draw from 30° on. The rules are in the head of `crates/core/src/glide_reach.rs`; the calls are `glide_reach(setting, terrain)` and `slope_classes(points, terrain)` (on the web `glideReach` and `slopeClasses`); when the answer is `cut_short` the shell loads the tiles covering a wider box and asks again. *A proposal for the screens, not decided:* a long press on the map offers "What a glide reaches from here" beside the forecast and "Add a site here" (on the web, a button in a place's sheet, since a mouse has no long press); the area is drawn as a filled area in a token colour at low opacity with the pack's line casing on its edge, a part of the edge that ends where the ground loaded ends dashed; a card under it carries the core's words and note and two settings, the glide (the pilot's, from the settings) and the start height ("On the ground" or a height in metres), each change asking again. Slope classes would be a layer of their own, drawn from a grid of places over the tiles on screen, its five colours for the owner to choose from the tokens.

In the cockpit a task adds one line above the line that says where to fly, on the map's page. It says where the flight stands: before the start the countdown to the gate with how far the edge of the start is and the speed that brings the glider there as the gate opens; then the pilot's clock; and on the right, all the while, the distance to goal along the shortest way and the glide that needs. The line below it is the same as without a task and shows the task's next turnpoint like any other place, with the point to fly to on its edge, so a pilot reads a turnpoint and a landing field the same way. A height to arrive over is shown for the goal and for the waypoints of a route, which are places to arrive at; for a cylinder on the way it is left out, because the point on its edge is not the place whose height the task gives. A place the pilot chooses still comes first; letting go of it goes back to the task. A tap on the task's line lists the turnpoints with what is reached, and has the two buttons for when the task and the flight have come apart: skip a turnpoint, or step back, which is also how a later start is taken.

On the map the task's cylinders and goal are thin lines in the map's ink, the one to fly to next heavier, and the shortest way still to fly a thin line through them. Ink, not a colour: colour belongs to the track, the wind and the airspace. The start taken, a turnpoint reached and the goal are spoken once.

## Open

- A dark map style. The map is light in dark mode today.
- On the web: a layout between the two, for a tablet held upright, where the phone's panel is wider than it needs to be; and the track at flown height in the 3D view.
- Landscape and iPad layouts for the flight screen.
- German. All text is English and written in the code; it moves to a string catalogue with the first translation.
- Units other than metric.
- Names for flights. A row is titled by its date until the sites data source exists and a flight can be named after its take-off.
- The device's clock. **Since 2026-11-22 the apps go by Venti's clock, the server's time, wherever they show an age or name "today"**, and no longer fall back to the device's, which can be days off, as a test run on Android showed (the core `7088731d`; Android `428c229d`, the web `3c58260c`, the iPhone `6ea073b2` and, for the forecast's own screen, `f2e39ceb`). Until Venti's clock has been read a shell hands the core 0, and the core then gives what is true by any clock: a wind station's reading shows its own time ("18:20") in place of an age and is not marked old; Today and the thermal map name every day by its weekday, call none "Today" or "Tomorrow" and leave no day or hour out, and the thermal map opens on the hour nearest one in the afternoon; on the iPhone the forecast's own screen calls no day "Today" and opens on the first (on Android and the web, which clock that screen goes by is to verify); the radar counts its ages from the moment in the server's answer; the ranking offers all years together; a league's deadline is named without calling the flight late. Comments' and notifications' ages show the day (since `b7e768c9`), and foehn's words the reading's time. **The exceptions:** the home-screen widget ages by the phone's clock, having no other ([The home-screen widget](#the-home-screen-widget)); on Android the assistant still reads the device's clock, as its source has it. On Android, why the server's time was never read in one run of 2026-11-22 is to verify.
